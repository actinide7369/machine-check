Check the app works (no belt needed)

The phone plays a pluck of a known note through its speaker and checks it hears the same note. Turn the volume up.

You can also hum a steady note, or pluck a rubber band stretched over an open box. These won't give a real belt tension, but they show the listening works.

Tension comes from the note using string physics, the same way Gates' sonic tension meters work. Thick, stiff belts ring a little higher than a perfect string, so the app can read slightly tight. Results are only as good as the span and mass you enter.

Formula: tension = 4 × mass per metre × span² × frequency². A plucked span vibrates like a string, so its lowest note (the fundamental) depends only on these three things.

The app waits for a sudden sound, skips the first 40 ms of the pluck, then takes about a third of a second of sound. It finds the repeat period of the waveform (the YIN pitch method), which still works when the phone's microphone barely picks up the lowest note.

Limits: phone microphones are weak below about 40 Hz, so very long or slack belts are hard to hear. Background noise can cause wrong readings, so pluck a few times and check the dots agree. The maker's range is yours to enter; the app doesn't know your belt.

Your test string

Tie a string to something fixed, run it over a table edge (a pencil makes a sharp edge) and hang a bottle on the end. The weight of the bottle is the true tension.

Work out the string mass by weighing a piece

Weigh a long piece, at least 5 m, so a kitchen scale is precise enough.

Try a few bottle weights. If the dots stay on the line, the app's physics and pitch detection are right. Changing the length at the same weight should change the note but not the tension.
